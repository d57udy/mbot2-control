# Rotational (heading) drift correction on small indoor wheeled robots (mBot2: CyberPi gyro + encoders + one forward ultrasonic)

## Gyro bias estimation (ZUPT / standstill re-estimation, temperature, MEMS numbers, recalibration cadence) and gyro + odometry fusion (Gyrodometry, complementary/Kalman)

### Takeaway
Cheap MEMS gyros drift on the order of 0.5 to 3 deg/min even after calibration, mostly from zero-rate offset (bias) that changes with temperature and motion. The standard fixes are (1) re-estimate bias whenever the robot is known to be stationary (zero angular rate update), and (2) Borenstein's "Gyrodometry": trust odometry heading by default and switch to gyro only in short intervals where the two disagree strongly (bumps, slips). Gyrodometry gave about 18x lower heading error than odometry alone and about 8x lower than gyro alone in a 140 s test.

### Cited Findings
**MEMS bias numbers**
- MPU-6886 (a 6-axis IMU in the class used on ESP32 boards like CyberPi; see Gaps on whether CyberPi uses it) datasheet: initial zero-rate output (ZRO) tolerance typ ±1 dps, max ±10 dps at 25 °C; ZRO variation vs temperature typ ±0.01 dps/°C, max ±0.05 dps/°C (-40 to +85 °C); rate noise 0.004 dps/√Hz typ (0.01 max) at 10 Hz; total RMS noise 0.04 dps typ at 100 Hz bandwidth; sensitivity scale factor initial tolerance ±1 %, variation over temperature ±2.5 %; cross-axis sensitivity ±1 % typ — [InvenSense MPU-6886 datasheet v1.1 (M5Stack mirror)](https://m5stack.oss-cn-shenzhen.aliyuncs.com/resource/docs/datasheet/core/MPU-6886-000193%2Bv1.1_GHIC_en.pdf)
- On an MPU6050 (DMP enabled) on a small differential robot, the gyro has an initial transient; "fully calibrated after 23 seconds of the robot being static, when drift becomes inferior to 0.5 deg/min". The author notes drift "will not be constant, but is instead greatly affected by the robot's motions"; rotational drift while moving was "in the order of 2 deg/min" — [Garcia-Saura 2015, Imperial College MSc thesis, arXiv:1509.02154](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.02154)
- Same thesis: drift rate measured as (yaw(t2) − yaw(t1)) / (t2 − t1), converted to deg/min; calibration routine places robot static in front of a wall while gyro drift is compensated, then calibrates motor maps using the distance sensor approaching the wall — [arXiv:1509.02154](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.02154)
- 1990s automotive-grade gyros (Murata Gyrostar, GEC START): raw drift 5 to 15 deg/min; after 5 min, heading errors of −59° (Gyrostar) and −70.8° (START); with an EKF error model these dropped to 3.8° and 12° (5 to 6x reduction), but "even with the EKF, a drift rate of 1 to 3°/min can still be expected" (Barshan and Durrant-Whyte 1995, as summarized by Borenstein & Feng) — [Borenstein & Feng 1996, Gyrodometry, ICRA](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated2/borenstien_gyro.pdf)
- MPU6050 hobby calibration (GoPiGo forum): 240 readings over one minute per calibration; gyro-z offset changed from 0.081 to 0.077 (sensor units) between two calibrations; board temperature 42.5 to 44.7 °C. No heading-error numbers reported; one user notes the robot "wandering a body width left or right after crossing the room" — [Dexter Industries forum](https://forum.dexterindustries.com/t/imu-explorations-mpu6050-calibration-drift/9292)

**Zero angular rate update (ZARU / ZUPT for yaw)**
- "Any time the device is known to be completely stationary, the gyroscope offset can be nulled to zero for that respective axis"; recurring rest states (idling car, stationary autonomous robot) can be used; ZRO algorithms "dynamically learn ZRO when the device is stable ... and continually refine this estimate"; a robot vacuum may need to "stop the robot periodically for ZRO calibration" because ZRO varies during a long run — [Analog Devices RAQ 139 / patent search summary](https://www.analog.com/en/resources/analog-dialogue/raqs/raq-issue-139.html) (page timed out on fetch; claims from search snippet, treat as secondary)
- Pausing integration when no motion is detected is a standard drift mitigation and is implemented by the MPU6050 DMP — [arXiv:1509.02154](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.02154)

**Makeblock specifics**
- mBot2 turning inaccuracy is attributed by Makeblock to "die-cut tolerance" of parts. Official fix: upload a calibration program, press A, robot performs a 720° rotation, waits for gyro readings to stabilize; coefficients are saved on the board and persist after power off. Alternative: a "turning coefficient" variable in the program — [Makeblock support: mBot2 Won't Turn Exactly as Expected](https://support.makeblock.com/hc/en-us/articles/15962163498903-23-mBot2-Won-t-Turn-Exactly-as-Expected)
- CyberPi Python API exposes `get_yaw()`, `reset_yaw()`, `reset_rotation(axis='all')` — [CyberPi Python API (lbotics mirror)](https://www.lbotics.at/images/mbot2/files/mBot2_API_cyberpi.pdf); [Makeblock: Detect rotation angles](https://support.makeblock.com/hc/en-us/articles/6553224171927-Case-6-Detect-the-Rotation-Angles-of-the-Gyroscope)

**Gyrodometry (Borenstein & Feng, ICRA 1996, pp. 423-428)**
- Rule: compute Δ_G−O = Δθ_gyro − Δθ_odo each sample (T = 0.1 s). If |Δ_G−O| > Δθ_thres (example 0.125°/T, i.e. 1.25°/s), integrate gyro increment for that sample, else integrate odometry increment — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated2/borenstien_gyro.pdf)
- Rationale: non-systematic odometry errors (bumps, cracks) act only during very short periods; then gyro and odometry differ significantly; otherwise they agree. In the single-bump example the threshold was exceeded for only 3 samples (0.3 s) yet that covered most of the gyro/odometry difference, so the gyro's drift barely enters the estimate — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated2/borenstien_gyro.pdf)
- Experiment: LabMate at 10 cm/s, 140 s run, 15 bumps (6, 9, 12 mm cables under right wheel, ~1 m spacing). Sonar wall-following reference accurate to ~±0.25°. Odometry accumulated −9° error; gyro (Gyrostar) was off ~4.2° (≈1.8°/min drift); Gyrodometry error stayed "well under ±0.5°", about 18x smaller than odometry-only and 8x smaller than gyro-only — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated2/borenstien_gyro.pdf)
- Each larger bump left a residual orientation error of 0.5 to 0.8° that odometry did not see; peak rotation rate during a bump ~7°/s (0.7°/0.1 s), so gyro drift (0.03°/s) was negligible relative to the transient (~2.4 %) — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated2/borenstien_gyro.pdf)
- Kalman-filter fusion requires a detailed model of sensors and wheel-floor interaction and cannot model "catastrophic" non-systematic events like bumps; Gyrodometry avoids that modelling — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated2/borenstien_gyro.pdf)

### Inferences
- For mBot2 with integer-degree yaw and ~120 ms round trip, a per-sample Gyrodometry test is too coarse (one LSB = 1° per 120 ms ≈ 8°/s, far above Borenstein's 1.25°/s threshold). A workable adaptation is per-segment: compare Δyaw_gyro vs Δθ_odo = (dR − dL)/b over each straight leg or each turn; if |diff| exceeds ~2° (two quantization steps) trust gyro for that segment, else trust odometry (or a weighted mix). Quantization of an absolute, on-device-integrated yaw does not accumulate (error stays within ±0.5°), so differencing absolute yaw readings over a segment is fine.
- ZARU in JS: whenever both encoder speeds are 0 for ≥1 to 2 s (e.g. during each stop before a sonar reading), record yaw at start and end of the stop; any change is pure bias. Estimate bias_rate = Δyaw/Δt over stops ≥10 s (needed to resolve a 1 to 2°/min drift with 1° quantization: 1°/(1.5°/min) ≈ 40 s for one count), low-pass it across stops (e.g. EMA α ≈ 0.2 to 0.3), and subtract bias_rate·dt from subsequent yaw. Alternatively, simply refuse to accept yaw changes while stationary (re-anchor heading offset at each stop).
- With MPU-6886-class typical temp coefficient 0.01 dps/°C, a 5 °C board warm-up shifts bias by 0.05 dps = 3°/min, which is the same order as the observed 1 to 2°/min. So warm-up (motors/ESP32 heating the CyberPi) is a plausible driver; recalibrating bias after a few minutes of operation and at each long stop is advised.
- Recalibration cadence: given ~2°/min moving drift and a goal of <2° heading error, a bias/heading check every ~1 min of driving is the right order of magnitude.

## Why floor transitions (carpet/parquet thresholds, rug edges) cause heading errors with differential drive, and how to detect/handle them

### Takeaway
A threshold or rug edge acts like Borenstein's "bump": one wheel travels extra vertical distance or slips, so the robot turns while encoders say it went straight, leaving a permanent orientation error. Carpets also add directional slip and castor/body drag, especially during in-place turns, so odometry over- or under-estimates rotation. Detection is via gyro-vs-odometry disagreement; iRobot patents estimate carpet drift exactly this way.

### Cited Findings
- When one wheel climbs a bump, it must travel extra ΔD to keep the robot straight; the controller keeps wheel rotations equal, so the robot curves toward the bump and leaves a constant orientation error Δθ_bump ≈ ΔD / b (b = wheelbase). The robot first turns toward the bump (wheel slows climbing) then away (rolling off) — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated2/borenstien_gyro.pdf)
- 6 to 12 mm cables produced 0.5 to 0.8° residual heading error per bump on a LabMate (wheelbase 340 mm per the UMBmark paper) — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated2/borenstien_gyro.pdf); [Borenstein & Feng 1996, IEEE T-RA](https://johnloomis.org/ece445/topics/odometry/borenstein/paper58.pdf)
- Odometry error sources listed by Borenstein: systematic (unequal wheel diameters, effective wheelbase uncertainty) and non-systematic (uneven floors, travel over unexpected objects, wheel slippage) — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated2/borenstien_gyro.pdf)
- iRobot carpet-drift patent: heading change due to carpet drift is "estimated by taking the difference between the change in heading computed from odometry and the change in heading computed from a gyroscopic sensor"; the robot rotates at least ~180° or ~360°; the odometry-minus-gyro difference vs heading approximates a sinusoid whose max/min give the carpet grain direction, and drift magnitude ≈ total drift / 2π over a full rotation; when aligned with carpet grain, odometry can under-estimate the turn and lag the gyro — [US9223312B2 (iRobot), Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9223312B2/en)
- Related iRobot family text (search snippet): on a thick plush rug, wheel odometry may indicate 1.4 m where actual translation was 1.0 m; slip typically occurs on carpets or rugs where additional resistance comes from the caster, body or wheel interaction, typically from the sides when turning in place; slip ratio depends on direction of travel — [US patent family "Carpet drift estimation"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9427875) (scanned PDF; numbers from search snippet, not verified in full text)
- Odometry correction can add a drift component proportional to grain magnitude in grain direction, "significantly" improving dead reckoning on carpet — [US patent 9969089 snippet](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9969089)

### Inferences
- mBot2 wheelbase is roughly 12 to 13 cm (not verified here). By Δθ ≈ ΔD/b, an extra 2 mm of effective travel on one wheel at a 5 to 8 mm parquet-to-carpet threshold gives ≈ 0.9 to 1°; much larger than on the 340 mm LabMate. Small wheelbases amplify bump-induced heading error, consistent with the owner's observation that transitions are worse.
- The gyro, not odometry, should be trusted across transitions (Gyrodometry logic). Conversely, if the gyro itself steps at transitions (vibration, tilt coupling into the yaw axis when the body pitches), the encoders are more trustworthy; a pitch/roll spike from the CyberPi accelerometer can flag "transition happening" and cause the system to prefer whichever source has agreed with the walls historically.
- Practical detection: (a) |Δyaw_gyro − Δθ_odo| over a short straight segment > ~2°; (b) pitch/roll transient > few degrees; (c) commanded vs measured wheel speed mismatch (motor load). After a flagged event, schedule a sonar wall-heading check (next section).
- In-place sweeps on carpet: odometry rotation is unreliable (side slip, castor drag). Use the gyro for the sweep angle, and compare to odometry to estimate carpet slip ratio per surface.

## Heading correction from the environment: Manhattan world / dominant wall direction, wall orientation from sonar sweeps, wall-following heading, Blanche

### Takeaway
Indoor walls are mostly parallel or perpendicular, so heading can be re-anchored modulo 90° by measuring the orientation of any wall relative to the robot and snapping it to the nearest of the four dominant directions. With sonar, wall orientation is best extracted from "regions of constant depth" (the perpendicular-return direction) in a rotating sweep, or from angle histograms / line fits of the sweep points. This bounds heading error to the measurement error (sub-degree to a few degrees) instead of letting it grow.

### Cited Findings
- Coughlan & Yuille's Manhattan world assumption (all dominant structures aligned with 3 orthogonal directions) is widely used in SLAM to neutralize drift; "soft Manhattan" variants treat near-parallel/near-orthogonal landmarks (within a threshold ε) as exactly parallel/orthogonal (Δθ_ideal ∈ {kπ/2}) while still allowing diagonal/curved features — [Han et al. 2024, SoMaSLAM, arXiv:2409.15736](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15736)
- SoMaSLAM targets sparse range sensing (few range points per scan) and reports lower absolute rotational and translational error than the non-Manhattan baseline (EG-SLAM) on standard datasets (e.g. Intel Lab) — [arXiv:2409.15736](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15736)
- Angle-histogram method (Weiß et al.; extended by Röfer 2002): compute orientation of the small line between each pair of neighbouring scan points; histogram these orientations; walls produce peaks; cross-correlating histograms of two scans gives their rotational offset. Röfer's extension uses line segmentation and sums segment lengths per orientation, determines "two main directions" to handle non-perpendicular environments, and correlates coarse-to-fine. The original approach of aligning "along the main direction ... only works in environments with perpendicular walls" — [Röfer 2002, Using Histogram Correlation to Create Consistent Laser Scan Maps, IROS](https://www.informatik.uni-bremen.de/kogrob/papers/iros02.pdf)
- Sonar-specific: "Sonar-based self-orientation of indoor mobile robots" (Univ. of Zagreb) estimates orientation by angle-histogram matching, with histograms derived via Hough transform from a sonar local occupancy grid; tested in a Pioneer 2DX simulator (no real-robot numbers in the abstract) — [ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/245610756_Sonar-based_self-orientation_of_indoor_mobile_robots)
- Wall-orientation-distribution approach: detect walls in depth measurements; assuming opposite walls parallel and adjacent walls orthogonal, maintain a distribution of possible wall orientations initialized uniform over −45° to +45° in robot frame, and correct odometry so observed walls align with the main orientation — [Clemson thesis (search snippet; full text 403)](https://open.clemson.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1248&context=all_theses)
- Orientation errors are the main concern because "once incurred, they grow without bound into lateral position errors"; extracting orientation from the environment to correct odometry heading eliminates the main source of position error — [Borenstein & Feng 1996, IEEE T-RA](https://johnloomis.org/ece445/topics/odometry/borenstein/paper58.pdf); [Extraction of Orientation from Floor Structure for Odometry Correction (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-540-45243-0_53)
- Sonar regions of constant depth (RCDs, Leonard & Durrant-Whyte): in a rotating scan, sequences of adjacent readings with nearly equal range form arcs; a smooth wall only returns an echo when the wall normal lies within the emission cone (beam width typically 20 to 30°). RCDs allow precise location of planes, corners, edges, cylinders — [Leonard 1992, Directed Sonar Sensing for Mobile Robot Navigation](http://web.cecs.pdx.edu/~mperkows/temp/KALMAN/KALMAN-PAPERS/sonar-kalman-ms%20thesis%20-%20kluwer1992.pdf); [Tardós et al., Robust Mapping and Localization using Sonar](http://diis.unizar.es/biblioteca/00/09/000973.pdf)
- Wall-following heading reference: two sideways Polaroid sonars measuring a continuous straight wall gave absolute orientation to "typically ±0.25°" (used as ground truth in Gyrodometry) — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated2/borenstien_gyro.pdf)
- Cox's Blanche (IEEE T-RA 7(2):193-204, 1991): low-cost office robot using only an optical rangefinder and odometry; odometry corrected by fast iterative matching of range data to an a-priori 2D line-segment map — [Springer chapter: Blanche](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4613-8997-2_17); [Röfer 2002 citing Cox](https://www.informatik.uni-bremen.de/kogrob/papers/iros02.pdf)

### Inferences (implementation for mBot2 sweeps, 70 to 85 readings per 360°, ~4.5° spacing)
- RCD / minimum-range method (recommended): in a 360° sweep, for each wall, the echo is strongest and the range minimal when the sensor points along the wall normal; with a 25 to 30° beam the wall appears as a plateau of near-constant range ~25 to 30° wide (≈6 readings). Find plateaus (adjacent readings within ~2 cm), take the centre angle (or fit r(φ) = d / cos(φ − φ_n) to the plateau edges) as the wall-normal bearing φ_n in robot frame. World wall normal = heading + φ_n, which should be ≡ 0 (mod 90°) in a Manhattan room. Heading correction = −wrap90(heading + φ_n), using the median over all detected walls. Expected accuracy is roughly half a reading step to a few degrees per wall; averaging 2 to 4 walls helps.
- Angle histogram alternative: convert sweep to points, compute neighbour-segment orientations (skip pairs with range jumps > ~10 cm or out of ~150 cm range), fold modulo 90°, weight by segment length, find the peak (1° bins, smoothing ±2°). Dominant direction minus the room's reference direction (taken from the first sweep at startup) = heading error. Because sonar points smear along an arc of the beam width, lines from wide-beam sonar are biased toward perpendicular-facing segments; the RCD centre method is more robust than raw point-line fits.
- Gate corrections: only apply if |correction| < ~15 to 20° (otherwise ambiguous snapping across 45°), at least two walls agree within ~3°, and plateau width matches the expected beam width (rejects corners and clutter). Fuse via complementary filter: heading ← heading + k·correction with k ≈ 0.5 to 1 per sweep, since the wall measurement is absolute but noisy.
- Straight-wall drive check: when driving parallel to a wall with the sensor not facing it, this robot cannot measure wall angle directly (single forward sensor). But pausing to turn toward a wall and finding the minimum-range bearing (a short ±20° mini-sweep, ~10 readings, ~1.2 s at 120 ms each) gives a cheap heading fix.
- Bootstrapping: define the room frame from the first sweep's dominant direction, so later corrections are just "keep walls at 0/90/180/270".

## Systematic odometry calibration (UMBmark, wheelbase and wheel diameter) and typical improvement

### Takeaway
UMBmark (bidirectional square path, 5 runs CW + 5 CCW) separates wheelbase error (Type A) from wheel-diameter mismatch (Type B) and yields correction factors Eb and Ed. It improved systematic odometry error by at least an order of magnitude (15x typical, up to 21x after a second iteration).

### Cited Findings
- Two dominant systematic errors in differential drive: unequal wheel diameters, Ed = D_R/D_L, and effective wheelbase uncertainty, Eb = b_actual/b_nominal; wheelbase uncertainty from tyre contact area "can be on the order of 1%" — [Borenstein & Feng 1996, IEEE T-RA 12(5)](https://johnloomis.org/ece445/topics/odometry/borenstein/paper58.pdf)
- Uni-directional square test is unsuitable (errors can cancel); bidirectional test: 4x4 m square, 5 runs CW and 5 CCW, record return position error; Emax,syst = max(r_cg,cw, r_cg,ccw) (distance of centre of gravity of end points from start) — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://johnloomis.org/ece445/topics/odometry/borenstein/paper58.pdf)
- Formulas (L = side length): β = (x_cg,cw − x_cg,ccw)/(−4L) · 180/π (Type B, degrees); α = (x_cg,cw + x_cg,ccw)/(−4L) · 180/π (Type A); R = (L/2)/sin(β/2); Ed = (R + b/2)/(R − b/2); b_actual = 90°/(90° − α) · b_nominal, i.e. Eb = 90/(90 − α). Equivalent y-based formulas exist; x and y estimates differed by <1 %. Wheel corrections are applied keeping the mean diameter unchanged — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://johnloomis.org/ece445/topics/odometry/borenstein/paper58.pdf)
- Result: Emax,syst 317 mm before vs 21 mm after (15-fold); calibration changed b from 340.00 to 336.17 mm (−1.1 %) and D_R/D_L from 1.00000 to 1.00084. One experiment improved only 6.4-fold (66 mm); after a second compensation, 20 mm (21-fold). Rule of thumb: re-iterate if Emax,syst > 3·SEM (SEM was 11.2 mm, σ ≈ 25 mm of return error). Robot travelled 0.2 m/s on legs to avoid slip — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://johnloomis.org/ece445/topics/odometry/borenstein/paper58.pdf)
- "Consistent improvement of at least one order of magnitude in odometric accuracy (with respect to systematic errors)" — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://johnloomis.org/ece445/topics/odometry/borenstein/paper58.pdf); [UMBmark page](http://www-personal.umich.edu/~johannb/umbmark.htm)
- Calibration alone "cannot guarantee trouble-free odometry" because of occasional bumps/slip, motivating Gyrodometry — [Borenstein & Feng 1996, Gyrodometry](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~motionplanning/papers/sbp_papers/integrated2/borenstien_gyro.pdf)
- Garcia-Saura self-calibrates a gyro-only robot using a wall and IR distance sensor (static bias compensation, motor dead-zone, max rotational speed, yaw PID, linear velocity map from approach speed to wall); resulting linear drift ~6 cm/m at 2 to 6 cm/s and ~9 cm/m at 6 to 10 cm/s — [arXiv:1509.02154](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1509.02154)

### Inferences
- For mBot2, scale UMBmark down to a 1x1 m or 1.5x1.5 m square on a single floor type (hard floor). Errors scale with path, so a small square still resolves Ed/Eb if end positions are measured to ~5 mm (tape on floor).
- With a gyro available, a simpler wheelbase calibration: do N in-place turns, compare Σ odometry rotation (dR − dL)/b to Σ gyro rotation; b_eff = b_nominal · θ_odo/θ_gyro. Do it per floor type, since effective wheelbase differs on carpet vs parquet. Wheel-diameter ratio: drive straight 1 to 2 m with wheel speeds commanded equal, measure gyro heading change; Ed follows from the arc (R = L/Δθ).
- Sonar can serve as the reference: drive straight toward a wall and compare encoder distance to sonar range change to calibrate mean wheel diameter (as Garcia-Saura did with IR).

### Gaps
- No mBot2-specific wheel diameter / wheelbase calibration data found beyond Makeblock's 720° gyro routine.

## How much heading error produces how much position error (quantifying the ~30 cm home error after ~2 minutes)

### Takeaway
Lateral error ≈ d · sin(Δθ): a constant 5° heading error over 3 m gives 26 cm. With a heading drift rate ω while driving at speed v for time T, lateral error grows quadratically, ≈ v·ω·T²/2; at 10 cm/s and 1.5°/min for 2 min that is ≈ 31 cm, matching the owner's observation.

### Cited Findings
- Orientation errors "grow without bound into lateral position errors"; in the square-path test systematic orientation errors translate directly into position errors — [Borenstein & Feng 1996, IEEE T-RA](https://johnloomis.org/ece445/topics/odometry/borenstein/paper58.pdf)
- A 1.1 % wheelbase error made a LabMate turn only 85° instead of 90° per corner in the example (Type A) — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://johnloomis.org/ece445/topics/odometry/borenstein/paper58.pdf)
- Uncalibrated systematic error 317 mm over a 16 m square path (≈2 %), 21 mm after calibration — [Borenstein & Feng 1996](https://johnloomis.org/ece445/topics/odometry/borenstein/paper58.pdf)

### Inferences (own calculations, geometry)
- Constant heading offset Δθ over straight distance d: lateral error e = d·sin Δθ. 5° over 3 m: 0.26 m; 5° over 1.5 m: 0.13 m; 2° over 3 m: 0.10 m; 1° per metre of travel ≈ 1.75 cm.
- Linearly growing heading error (constant drift ω, speed v, time T): e ≈ v·ω·T²/2 (small angles). Example: v = 0.10 m/s, ω = 1.5°/min (0.026 rad/min), T = 120 s: path 12 m, final heading error 3°, lateral error ≈ 0.31 m. At 1°/min ≈ 0.21 m; at 2°/min ≈ 0.42 m.
- For a closed loop returning home, a constant heading offset applied from the start rotates the whole path about the start point and does not by itself create home error; what matters is heading error introduced during the loop (drift, bumps at transitions). One sudden 3° error at a rug edge 2 m into a 4 m loop, with ~2 m remaining, gives ≈ 2 × sin 3° ≈ 10 cm at home. Several transitions plus drift plausibly sum to ~30 cm.
- Required correction budget: to keep home error under ~5 cm on a ~12 m excursion, average heading error must stay below about 0.5 to 1°, which implies a wall-based heading fix roughly every 1 to 3 m of travel or every ~30 to 60 s, plus a fix after each detected floor transition.

### Gaps
- mBot2 typical driving speed and path geometry in the owner's run are assumed (10 cm/s); recompute with actual speed.
