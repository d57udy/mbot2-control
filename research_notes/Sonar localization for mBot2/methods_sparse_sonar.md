# Localization and SLAM methods for sparse, wide-beam ultrasonic range data on a small indoor robot

Target setup: one forward HC-SR04-class sonar (~25-30 deg beam, reliable to ~150 cm, ~8 readings/s while spinning, specular dropouts beyond ~55 deg incidence), ~70-85 readings per 360 deg in-place sweep, wheel encoders + gyro, browser JavaScript compute (a few ms to tens of ms per update).

Note on sourcing: primary PDFs for Fox et al. 1999 (MCL), Yap & Shelton 2009 (sonar SLAM), Zhou et al. 2022 (sparse graph SLAM), Han et al. 2024 (SoMaSLAM), Olson 2009 (correlative scan matching), Censi 2008 (PL-ICP) and Moravec & Elfes 1985 were read in full text this session. Tardós 2002, Konolige 1997, Leonard & Durrant-Whyte 1992, Thrun 2003, Beevers & Huang 2006 and Fox KLD-sampling are cited from abstracts/search summaries only. Statements drawn from general knowledge of Probabilistic Robotics (Thrun, Burgard, Fox 2005) that were not re-verified this session are labelled as such and placed under Inferences.

## 1. Monte Carlo localization (particle filters) with sonar beam models

### Takeaway
MCL is the best-established method for localizing against a known map with sonar: the original MCL work used robots with 7 to 24 sonars and found 1,000 to 5,000 particles optimal under real-time constraints, and KLD-sampling cut global-localization sample needs from ~50,000 to ~3,000 on sonar data. For a single sonar in a small room, a likelihood-field style model evaluated on a precomputed distance grid, with max-range/no-echo readings handled explicitly, is the most practical fit for browser JS.

### Cited Findings
- MCL represents belief as N weighted particles; it adds a small number of uniformly distributed random samples after each step, which the authors call "essential for relocalization" after failures (kidnapping). — [Fox, Burgard, Dellaert, Thrun, AAAI-99](http://robots.stanford.edu/papers/fox.aaai99.pdf)
- Experiments used B21, B18 and Pioneer robots (RHINO, Minerva, Robin, Marian) "equipped with arrays of sonar sensors (from 7 to 24)", one or two lasers, and on Minerva a ceiling camera; all evaluated under run-time conditions. — [Fox et al. 1999](http://robots.stanford.edu/papers/fox.aaai99.pdf)
- MCL gave more accurate localization than grid-based Markov localization (for both sonar and laser) with an order of magnitude less memory and computation. Grid sizes below 8 cm could not be updated in real time. Under real-time conditions, the "optimal" sample set size was between 1,000 and 5,000 samples; grid-based localization needed ~4 cm grids to match that accuracy, and a 20 cm grid required ~10x the memory of MCL with 5,000 samples. During global localization a single scan took up to 120 s with the grid approach vs consistently < 3 s for MCL (1999 hardware). — [Fox et al. 1999](http://robots.stanford.edu/papers/fox.aaai99.pdf)
- Adaptive sampling performed about equally to fixed sample sets in normal tracking, but was superior when uncertainty varies widely (global vs local, or after artificially injected 180 +/- 90 deg / 200 cm pose errors). — [Fox et al. 1999](http://robots.stanford.edu/papers/fox.aaai99.pdf)
- KLD-sampling adapts particle count by bounding the KL-divergence between the sample-based and true posterior. In sonar-based global localization in a 54 m x 18 m office, fixed-size filters needed about 50,000 samples to reach KL < 0.25, while KLD-sampling reached the same level with ~3,000 samples on average. — [Fox, KLD-Sampling (NIPS / IJRR 2003)](http://papers.neurips.cc/paper/1998-kld-sampling-adaptive-particle-filters.pdf); [IJRR 2003 version](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0278364903022012001)
- With vision-only data on a fast, bumpy museum run (700 m path, up to 163 cm/s), MCL errors sometimes exceeded 1 m but it recovered via multiple hypotheses, while grid Markov localization lost track. (Shows MCL's robustness to weak sensing, not sonar accuracy.) — [Fox et al. 1999](http://robots.stanford.edu/papers/fox.aaai99.pdf)
- Max-range handling in a real sonar system: Yap & Shelton exclude measurements equal to the sonar maximum range from particle weighting ("Missing sonar measurements are equal to s_max and are not considered in computing the match value"), using a range std. dev. of 50 mm. — [Yap & Shelton, ICRA 2009](https://www.cs.ucr.edu/~cshelton/papers/docs/ICRA2009.pdf)

### Inferences
- (General knowledge of Probabilistic Robotics ch. 6, not re-verified this session.) The beam model is a mixture of four densities per reading: Gaussian "hit" around the ray-cast expected range, exponential "short" (unexpected obstacles), a point mass at max range, and uniform "random". It needs ray casting per particle per beam, which is costly and makes the likelihood non-smooth in pose. The likelihood-field model instead projects the beam endpoint and looks up the distance to the nearest obstacle in a precomputed distance grid; it is much cheaper, smooth, and typically discards max-range readings. For sonar specifically, the textbook notes the likelihood field ignores the cone and free-space information.
- For a 1 sonar / 70-85 readings per sweep setup, the cost of a likelihood-field update is roughly N_particles x N_readings table lookups. At 2,000 particles x 80 readings = 160k lookups per sweep, which is well within a few ms to tens of ms in JS. Incremental per-reading updates (8 Hz) are even cheaper (2,000 lookups each). This is an estimate, not a measured figure.
- Wide cone: a cheap approximation is to evaluate several rays across the cone (e.g. -12, 0, +12 deg) and take the minimum expected range or the max likelihood, since an HC-SR04 returns the nearest echo anywhere in the cone. This is a common practical fix; I found no quantitative study of it for HC-SR04 in this session.
- Specular/no-echo: treat readings at or beyond ~150 cm (or timeouts) as "no information" or give them a weak max-range likelihood instead of a strong "free" assertion, and inflate sigma or down-weight readings where the map predicts high incidence angle (>~55 deg). Because consecutive readings in a sweep are correlated (overlapping cones), down-weighting each reading (e.g. raising likelihoods to a power < 1) avoids overconfident collapse.
- Known-map localization with a single sonar in a symmetric rectangular room will be ambiguous (multi-modal); MCL's multi-hypothesis nature is the main reason to prefer it over EKF here.

- I did not retrieve the specific cm-level error numbers from Fox et al.'s sonar curves (the figures plot "Average estimation error [cm]" vs samples, but values were not extractable as text).

## 2. Sonar-specific occupancy and inverse sensor models (Elfes/Moravec, Konolige MURIEL, Thrun forward models)

### Takeaway
The classic Moravec & Elfes model spreads "empty" evidence over the whole cone up to the range and "occupied" evidence on a thin arc at the range, weighted by a quadratic angular falloff; specular readings were handled crudely by discarding readings near max range. Konolige's MURIEL adds explicit specularity modelling and "pose buckets" to stop redundant readings from the same pose overcounting, which is directly relevant to a spin-in-place sweep. Thrun's forward-model EM mapping resolves the conflicts of independent-cell inverse models but is offline.

### Cited Findings
- Moravec & Elfes used 24 Polaroid transducers spaced 15 deg apart, beam angle 30 deg at -38 dB, useful range 0.9 to 35 ft, ~1% range accuracy. — [Moravec & Elfes 1985, "High Resolution Maps from Wide Angle Sonar"](https://frc.ri.cmu.edu/~hpm/project.archive/robot.papers/1985/al2.html)
- Each reading assigns "probably EMPTY" to the cone interior and "somewhere OCCUPIED" to a narrow band at the measured range; angular weight follows 1 - (2*theta/omega)^2 across the beam width omega. Empty evidence combined by probabilistic addition (Emp := Emp + Emp_k - Emp*Emp_k); occupied probabilities are first reduced in cells marked empty, then combined. — [Moravec & Elfes 1985](https://frc.ri.cmu.edu/~hpm/project.archive/robot.papers/1985/al2.html)
- Specularity: "Range readings above a certain maximum R_u are discarded. We observe that sonar readings caused by specular reflections are often near the maximum range." — [Moravec & Elfes 1985](https://frc.ri.cmu.edu/~hpm/project.archive/robot.papers/1985/al2.html)
- Map-to-map matching with 6-inch cells gave about 6 inches displacement and 3 deg rotation accuracy, ~1 s of VAX time for 3,000-cell maps from ~200 readings. — [Moravec & Elfes 1985](https://frc.ri.cmu.edu/~hpm/project.archive/robot.papers/1985/al2.html)
- Konolige (1997) argues refined sensor models (including specularity) and independence assumptions are crucial for occupancy grids, and introduces MURIEL (MUltiple Representation, Independent Evidence Log), which "can dramatically improve the fidelity of occupancy grid map-making in specular and real-time environments". — [Konolige, Autonomous Robots 4:351-367, 1997](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1008806422571); [Semantic Scholar summary](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Improved-Occupancy-Grids-for-Map-Building-Konolige/321bf625c5da898b5bce15b816077f6348aefe6d)
- MURIEL introduced "pose buckets" to deal with redundant information (repeated readings from the same pose/angle), and handled specular readings by probabilistic inference. — [Experimental comparison of sonar occupancy grid methods (summary)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/293076408_Experimental_Comparison_of_Sonar_Based_Occupancy_Grid_Mapping_Methods)
- Thrun (2003) notes standard occupancy mapping decomposes into independent per-cell problems, which "induces conflicts that may lead to inconsistent maps, even for noise-free sensors"; his method uses forward sensor models and EM in the full map space, giving more accurate maps. It is an offline method. — [Thrun, "Learning Occupancy Grid Maps with Forward Sensor Models", Autonomous Robots 15, 2003](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1025584807625)
- In a sonar RBPF comparison, grid maps suffer "data smearing": "measurements are often blurred onto a region of the map to account for angular and distance uncertainty", and grid-based RBPF required per-environment parameter tuning, unlike a line-segment map. — [Yap & Shelton, ICRA 2009](https://www.cs.ucr.edu/~cshelton/papers/docs/ICRA2009.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a spin-in-place sweep with ~8 Hz readings and a ~25-30 deg cone, consecutive readings overlap by roughly 25 deg of beam each; a MURIEL-style pose bucket (count at most one reading per cell per angular bin per stop location) would prevent the 70-85 readings of a sweep from over-asserting walls or free space.
- Practical evidence-fusion recipe consistent with the above: log-odds grid; strong "free" only along the cone interior well short of the return; "occupied" arc only near the return and weighted toward the beam axis; skip or weakly weight readings at/over ~150 cm or timeouts (specular candidates); clamp log-odds to keep the map revisable.
- The 1985 result (6 in / 3 deg from ~200 readings with 24 sonars) is a useful ballpark for what a ~200-reading map-match can achieve, but those were higher-quality Polaroid sensors with longer range.

### Gaps
- No quantitative MURIEL vs Elfes accuracy numbers were retrieved (full Konolige text not fetched).
- No study found on the HC-SR04 specifically; beam width and specular cutoff for HC-SR04 must come from adjacent research or measurement.

## 3. Feature-based sonar SLAM (RCDs, Tardós et al. 2002 Hough, Kleeman & Kuc)

### Takeaway
Feature-based sonar methods exploit sonar physics: in a dense rotating scan, a wall or corner produces an arc of near-constant range (an RCD), and the true reflection direction is near the arc's centre/perpendicular. Tardós et al. used Hough voting over grouped sonar returns to extract lines and corners robustly. A single 360 deg sweep from one sonar can in principle yield RCDs and orthogonal wall lines, but sparse data generally needs grouping of several scans and strong priors (orthogonality).

### Cited Findings
- Leonard & Durrant-Whyte identify circular arcs, "regions of constant depth" (RCDs), as "the most natural and reliable features contained in sonar scans" from Polaroid sonar; they give algorithms to predict RCDs for plane, cylinder, corner and edge features and to explain unexpected RCDs as new features. — [Leonard & Durrant-Whyte, "Sonar interpretation using regions of constant depth", IEEE](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/180885); [Directed Sonar Sensing for Mobile Robot Navigation (1992 book PDF)](http://web.cecs.pdx.edu/~mperkows/temp/KALMAN/KALMAN-PAPERS/sonar-kalman-ms%20thesis%20-%20kluwer1992.pdf)
- Tardós, Neira, Newman & Leonard (IJRR 21(4):311-330, 2002) build feature-based stochastic maps with standard Polaroid sonar, contributing (1) a perceptual grouping process (Hough transform) for robust identification of straight segments and corners "from sparse and noisy sonar data", (2) map joining of independent limited-size local maps into a globally consistent map, and (3) robust data association. — [Tardós et al. 2002, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/027836402320556340); [ResearchGate](https://researchgate.net/publication/2495107_Robust_Mapping_and_Localization_in_Indoor_Environments_Using_Sonar_Data)
- Yap & Shelton extract lines from groups of m (e.g. 15) consecutive 16-sonar scans with a randomized Hough transform (delta-rho = 50 mm, delta-theta = 1 deg; accumulator threshold e.g. 100; at least N0 = 8 points and 200 mm length per segment; break at gaps > 500 mm; snap to horizontal/vertical within 5 deg). They report that "because of the Hough transform, our line extraction procedure is quite robust to noise caused by specular and multiple reflections and phantom readings". — [Yap & Shelton, ICRA 2009](https://www.cs.ucr.edu/~cshelton/papers/docs/ICRA2009.pdf)
- Yap & Shelton plot each sonar return as a point along the transducer's central axis, explicitly accepting the 20-30 deg angular uncertainty and relying on Hough voting to reject wrong points; of 16 readings per scan, "only a small fraction actually corresponds to correct measurements". — [Yap & Shelton, ICRA 2009](https://www.cs.ucr.edu/~cshelton/papers/docs/ICRA2009.pdf)
- Without the orthogonality assumption, "the resulting maps and robot trajectories are not properly estimated and corrected". — [Yap & Shelton, ICRA 2009](https://www.cs.ucr.edu/~cshelton/papers/docs/ICRA2009.pdf)

### Inferences
- A 70-85 reading in-place sweep is exactly the "dense rotating scan" situation RCD theory was built for: each flat wall within range should appear as an RCD whose minimum-range direction points along the wall normal; the wall's distance is the RCD's range and its orientation is the RCD's central bearing. This gives one line (rho, theta) per visible wall from a single sweep, avoiding the beam-axis smearing error. Corners produce RCDs too, but are distinguishable from walls only with motion (two viewpoints) or with the expectation from a map.
- With ~150 cm reliable range and >55 deg incidence dropouts, only walls nearly perpendicular to the beam within 1.5 m will return; expect 1-4 usable RCDs per sweep in a typical room. With an orthogonality prior and gyro heading, two perpendicular walls fully fix x, y, theta up to the room's symmetry.
- A Hough accumulator over (rho, theta) with ~80 points is trivial compute in JS (sub-ms for a coarse 5 cm x 2 deg grid).
- Kleeman & Kuc's sonar classification (plane / corner / edge from multi-transducer timing) requires multiple receivers; with a single HC-SR04 it is not directly applicable. (Not verified from primary source this session.)

### Gaps
- Tardós 2002 quantitative accuracy (e.g. map error) not retrieved; only abstract-level claims.
- Kleeman & Kuc papers not retrieved; claims about their multi-transducer requirement are from background knowledge.
- No primary source found reporting line extraction from one rotating low-cost sonar sweep with accuracy numbers.

## 4. Scan matching for sparse data (ICP, PL-ICP, correlative, NDT)

### Takeaway
Point-to-point and point-to-line ICP assume overlapping, accurately bearing-resolved points; with ~10-60 noisy beams they degrade sharply, and with wide-beam sonar the bearing of each point is uncertain by +/- 12-15 deg, which violates their core assumption. Correlative scan-to-map matching (Olson 2009) is far more robust to poor initialization and maps naturally onto a lookup-table likelihood, but sparse data produces flat, ambiguous score surfaces; papers on sparse sensing report it needs modification (neighbourhood matching) for loop closure. For this robot, correlative matching of a whole sweep against a likelihood/distance grid is the most suitable member of this family.

### Cited Findings
- PL-ICP uses a point-to-line metric with exact closed-form minimization; it converges quadratically and in a finite number of steps (vs linear for vanilla ICP), "is more precise, and requires less iterations", but "is less robust to very large initial displacement errors", and also less robust than IDC and ICP to big rotational displacements. It uses a trimming procedure to eliminate outliers. — [Censi, ICRA 2008](https://censi.science/pub/research/2008-icra-plicp.pdf)
- Olson's correlative matcher evaluates p(z|x,m) over a 3D (dx, dy, dtheta) window using a precomputed raster table of log-probabilities; the multi-resolution version uses a 3 cm high-res and 30 cm low-res table (each low-res cell = max of its high-res cells) with branch-and-bound so it gives results identical to brute force. — [Olson, ICRA 2009](https://april.eecs.umich.edu/pdfs/olson2009icra.pdf)
- Olson reports ICP and ICL are fast but their quality is "completely unacceptable" in high-uncertainty regimes; ICP took 56 / 99 / 145 ms for 0.5 m-20 deg / 2 m-40 deg / 4 m-90 deg windows; the multi-resolution correlative matcher runs in 8.4 ms in the incremental case (Core2 2.4 GHz, single thread, lidar, 1/32 m grid, 1 deg theta steps) and handles 4 m / 90 deg uncertainty at over 10 Hz. — [Olson, ICRA 2009](https://april.eecs.umich.edu/pdfs/olson2009icra.pdf)
- Zhou et al. (2022) state that pose-graph front-ends normally assume consecutive scans "have significant overlaps so that a rigid-body transformation can be calculated directly. However, sparse sensing invalidates this assumption." They also find correlative scan-to-map matching on sparse, uncertain data "tends to fail frequently at differentiating the correct matches from the incorrect ones because their scores are similar", and propose an approximate match heuristic that matches each point to a neighbourhood of cells rather than a single cell. — [Zhou, Hu, Liu, Khan, IROS 2022](https://www.sihangliu.com/docs/iros2022.pdf)
- Hector SLAM and Cartographer "do not produce meaningful output under moderately sparse settings (e.g., less than 60 range measurements)"; GMapping fails under 30 range measurements per scan (Intel Lab subsampling from 180). — [Zhou et al., IROS 2022](https://www.sihangliu.com/docs/iros2022.pdf)
- SoMaSLAM reports a failure mode where lack of data accumulates drift and then "the correlative scan-to-map matching score" degrades so loop closures are missed (MIT Killian, 4 points). — [Han et al., SoMaSLAM, arXiv 2409.15736](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.15736)

### Inferences
- With a single sonar, each sweep point's bearing is uncertain by roughly half the beam width, so point-to-line ICP correspondences are systematically biased (walls appear bowed/arc-shaped). Using RCD-derived lines or only beam-axis points near RCD centres before ICP would reduce this bias; alternatively, match the raw sweep with a cone-aware likelihood.
- Correlative matching is the best fit here: a 2D distance/likelihood table of a room (e.g. 3 m x 3 m at 2 cm = 22,500 cells) and a search window of e.g. +/-20 cm, +/-10 deg at 2 cm / 1 deg steps is ~21x21x21 = 9,261 poses x 80 points = ~0.74M lookups, roughly a few to ~10 ms in JS, and a coarse-to-fine variant reduces that further. This is a back-of-envelope estimate.
- Use a robust kernel in scoring (e.g. clamp per-point log-likelihood at a floor, as the likelihood-field model does with its random component) so specular/no-echo points cannot dominate.
- NDT was not researched in depth; with ~80 points over a room, most NDT cells would have too few points to estimate a covariance, so NDT is likely a poor fit unless cells are large.

## 5. FastSLAM / GMapping / graph SLAM with sparse sensing (Beevers & Huang multiscans, Yap & Shelton, Zhou et al., SoMaSLAM)

### Takeaway
Every successful sparse-sensing SLAM system found groups consecutive sparse scans into a "multiscan" (Beevers & Huang 2006), extracts line segments from it, and leans on an orthogonal/Manhattan prior. RBPF with line maps worked with 16-sonar rings using 200-300 particles; graph SLAM with line landmarks works down to 4 beams per scan and runs at ~1 ms per update on a desktop, whereas GMapping fails below ~30 beams. For a single sonar doing in-place sweeps, each sweep is already a natural multiscan, so a line-landmark graph or small RBPF is feasible, but full SLAM is likely overkill if the room map can be built once.

### Cited Findings
- Beevers & Huang (ICRA 2006, pp. 2285-2290) address SLAM where sensing provides "too little data to extract features from a single scan", grouping several scans taken while moving into "multiscans", trading higher data density for greater measurement uncertainty from odometry error; implemented with a Rao-Blackwellized particle filter. — [Beevers & Huang, "SLAM with sparse sensing", ICRA 2006](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/abstract/document/1642043)
- Beevers' robots used an array of five short-range infrared rangefinders; his thesis includes a working particle-filter SLAM implementation on an 8-bit microcontroller with a small IR rangefinder array. — [Beevers PhD abstract, RPI](https://www.cs.rpi.edu/research/phdabstracts/beevers.html)
- Yap & Shelton (ICRA 2009): P3-DX with 16 sonars (two rings of 8; 20 deg spacing; 20-30 deg angular uncertainty), RBPF where each particle holds the recent m poses (e.g. 15) and a single line-segment map; weights from a ray-cast match value excluding max-range readings; selective resampling when N_eff < N/2. Results: three environments, 6.7 m x 6.7 m (200 particles, 26.7 m travelled, 10.9 valid readings/step), 38.6 m x 12.7 m (300 particles, 85.7 m), and 70.0 m x 53.7 m (200 particles, 283.5 m, 9.7 valid readings/step); loops closed in all three. A grid-based RBPF baseline showed smearing and needed per-environment tuning. — [Yap & Shelton, ICRA 2009](https://www.cs.ucr.edu/~cshelton/papers/docs/ICRA2009.pdf)
- Zhou et al. (IROS 2022) graph SLAM for sparse sensing: multiscan from the k previous scans transformed into the current frame, split-and-merge line extraction, line landmarks in polar form, landmark graph for odometry plus pose graph with modified correlative loop closure. Absolute translational error with 11 beams (of 180): Aces 0.0455 +/- 0.0492, Intel 0.0848 +/- 0.1151, MIT Killian 0.0718 +/- 0.1913; GMapping with 30 beams: 0.1040 +/- 0.2839 (Aces), 0.1139 +/- 0.2274 (Intel), failed on Killian. Mean processing time ~0.8 ms per update (max front-end 11 ms, max back-end 106 ms) on an i7-9700K, ~1/100 of the 0.185 s data interval. Real-world test: Crazyflie with 4 VL53L1x ToF sensors (front/back/left/right, ~2 m range, 10 Hz) produced maps close to the floor plan. — [Zhou et al., IROS 2022](https://www.sihangliu.com/docs/iros2022.pdf)
- Zhou et al. conclude GMapping "spends significant amount of time ... due to need for many particles" and that "graph-based approaches are more suited for sparse sensing than particle based methods". — [Zhou et al., IROS 2022](https://www.sihangliu.com/docs/iros2022.pdf)
- SoMaSLAM (2024) extends the Zhou et al. graph with soft Manhattan-world landmark-landmark constraints; with 4 of 180 points on Intel Lab, EG-SLAM had ~20x the translational and ~5x the rotational error of SoMaSLAM; with 11 points on Aces SoMaSLAM's translational error was 0.05 +/- 0.05. — [Han et al., SoMaSLAM, arXiv 2409.15736](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.15736)

### Inferences
- The robot's 360 deg in-place sweep (~10 s at 8 Hz) is a multiscan with near-zero translational odometry error (rotation only, gyro-corrected), which is a better situation than the moving multiscans in Beevers/Yap/Zhou. That suggests "stop, sweep, extract lines/RCDs, update" is a sound cycle.
- Valid readings per step in Yap & Shelton (~10 per 16-sonar scan) are comparable to what a single sonar gives in ~1.3 s; their 200-particle RBPF running on a 2005-era ThinkPad suggests a 100-300 particle line-map RBPF is within browser JS budget, though no timing was reported in the paper.
- For a small known room, a pragmatic stack is: (a) build the map once (occupancy grid with sonar inverse model, or hand-entered wall lines); (b) localize with MCL using a likelihood-field/cone-aware model; (c) optionally refine the MCL mean with correlative sweep-to-map matching or RCD-line fitting. Full SLAM (graph or RBPF with line landmarks plus Manhattan prior) is warranted only if the map is unknown or changes.
